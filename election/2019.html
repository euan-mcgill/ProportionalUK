Overview

This election, even moreso than in 2017, was a referendum on completing the "Brexit" process - in other words finding any means possible for the UK to officially leave the European Union. It was also a highly-polarised election which pitted great personalities and ideologies against each other.

There was a lot of tactical voting in this election, a major factor in first-past-the-post (or "winner takes all") elections. With the proportional approach in fixed-size districts, the Conservatives would still win a majority - albeit with a much slimmer majority than in the real election that year. However, multi-member constituencies still provide a more proportional outcome with more voter's choices being represented in parliament.

Notes

On one side, the Conservatives and their highly-recognisable Boris Johnson proclaimed that Brexit would be quickly implemented come what may with a large enough mandate - so much so that Nigel Farage's Brexit Party stood aside in all seats hitherto held by Conservative MPs. On the other (in England and Wales), the Labour party led by Jeremy Corbyn focused more on living standards and upturning the prevailing economic ideology in the UK since at least 1979.

Owing to Brexit fatigue, the Conservatives were more successful than in 2017, where the election was much more close-run (especially in terms of the proportion of votes won. Moreover, Labour lost out more in Remain-voting Scotland where this fatigue pushed more voters towards independence-supporting parties.
